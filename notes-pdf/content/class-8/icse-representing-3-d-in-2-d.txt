# Two and three dimensions
- A **plane (2-D) figure** has length and breadth only: triangle, rectangle, circle.
- A **solid (3-D) figure** has length, breadth and height: cube, cylinder, sphere.
- On paper a solid can be shown by an **oblique sketch** (on squared paper, not to exact scale) or an **isometric sketch** (on isometric dot paper, with true lengths along three directions).

# Faces, edges and vertices
- A **polyhedron** is a solid bounded only by flat polygonal faces.
- **Face**: a flat surface. **Edge**: a line segment where two faces meet. **Vertex**: a point where three or more edges meet.
- Cylinders, cones and spheres have curved surfaces, so they are **not** polyhedra.

# Prisms and pyramids
- A **prism** has two congruent, parallel polygonal ends joined by rectangular (or parallelogram) faces. It is named after its ends.
- A **pyramid** has a polygonal base and triangular faces meeting at one vertex (the apex). It is named after its base.
| Solid with an $n$-sided base | Faces | Vertices | Edges |
| Prism | $n + 2$ | $2n$ | $3n$ |
| Pyramid | $n + 1$ | $n + 1$ | $2n$ |
?eg Find the number of faces, vertices and edges of an octagonal prism.
= $n = 8$
=> 10 faces, 16 vertices, 24 edges
?eg Find the number of faces, vertices and edges of a pentagonal pyramid.
=> 6 faces, 6 vertices, 10 edges

# Euler's formula
- For every polyhedron:
$$F + V - E = 2$$
- It can be used to find one of the three numbers when the other two are known, or to check whether a polyhedron with given numbers can exist.
?eg A polyhedron has 8 faces and 12 vertices. How many edges does it have?
= $8 + 12 - E = 2$
=> 18
?eg A polyhedron has 20 edges and 12 vertices. Find the number of faces.
= $F + 12 - 20 = 2$
=> 10
?eg Can a polyhedron have 10 faces, 20 edges and 15 vertices?
= $10 + 15 - 20 = 5$, which is not 2.
=> No
?eg Verify Euler's formula for a square pyramid.
= $F = 5$, $V = 5$, $E = 8$: $5 + 5 - 8$
=> 2, so the formula holds

# Nets
- A **net** is a plane figure that can be folded to form a solid.
- A cube has 6 square faces, so each of its nets has 6 squares. There are 11 different nets of a cube.
- Not every arrangement of 6 squares is a net: four squares meeting at a point, or six squares in one row, will not fold into a cube.

| Solid | Its net is made of |
| Cube | 6 squares |
| Cuboid | 6 rectangles in three equal pairs |
| Triangular prism | 2 triangles and 3 rectangles |
| Square pyramid | 1 square and 4 triangles |
| Tetrahedron | 4 triangles |
| Cylinder | 2 circles and 1 rectangle |
| Cone | 1 circle and 1 sector of a circle |
- In a **die**, the numbers on opposite faces add up to 7: 1 and 6, 2 and 5, 3 and 4. In a net of a die, opposite faces are never next to each other.
?eg In the cross-shaped net of a cube shown above, the four squares in the column are numbered 1, 2, 3, 4 from the top and the side squares are 5 (left) and 6 (right). Which faces are opposite each other?
= In a column of four, the first and third are opposite, and so are the second and fourth. The two side squares are opposite each other.
=> 1 and 3; 2 and 4; 5 and 6
?eg What solid is formed by a net of two equal circles and a rectangle?
=> A cylinder

# Views of a solid
- A solid looks different from different directions. The three standard views are the **front view**, the **side view** and the **top view** (plan).
| Solid (standing upright) | Front view | Side view | Top view |
| Cube | Square | Square | Square |
| Cuboid | Rectangle | Rectangle | Rectangle |
| Cylinder | Rectangle | Rectangle | Circle |
| Cone | Triangle | Triangle | Circle with its centre marked |
| Sphere | Circle | Circle | Circle |
| Square pyramid | Triangle | Triangle | Square with its diagonals |
- A **cross-section** is the shape obtained when a solid is cut by a plane. A cube cut parallel to a face gives a square; a cylinder cut parallel to its base gives a circle, and cut vertically through its axis gives a rectangle.
?eg Three cubes are placed in a row. Describe the front, side and top views.
=> Front: 3 squares in a row. Side: 1 square. Top: 3 squares in a row.
?eg What is the cross-section when a cone is cut by a plane parallel to its base?
=> A circle

# Common mistakes
- Counting hidden edges or vertices twice, or missing them.
- Applying Euler's formula to a cylinder, cone or sphere.
- Mixing up the formulas for prisms and pyramids.
- Assuming that any six joined squares form a net of a cube.
- Confusing the top view with the front view.
- Writing Euler's formula as $F + E - V = 2$.

A: 5 faces, 9 edges, 6 vertices

A: 8, 12 and 18

A: A hexagon; 12 edges

A: 8
Q: A polyhedron has 9 faces and 9 vertices. Find the number of edges.
A: 16
Q: Can a polyhedron have 7 faces, 10 vertices and 16 edges?
A: No; $7 + 10 - 16 = 1$
Q: Name the solid whose net has one square and four triangles.
A: A square pyramid
Q: On a die, which number is opposite 3?
A: 4
Q: Write the front, side and top views of an upright cylinder.
A: Rectangle, rectangle, circle
Q: Is a cone a polyhedron? Give a reason.
A: No; it has a curved surface
