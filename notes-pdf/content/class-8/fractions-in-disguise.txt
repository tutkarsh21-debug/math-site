@title Fractions in Disguise
@summary Percentages are fractions with denominator 100. This chapter covers converting between fractions, decimals and percentages, finding a percentage of a quantity, percentage change, profit and loss, discount, tax, and growth with and without compounding.

# Per cent
- **Per cent** means "out of a hundred". $x\% = \dfrac{x}{100}$.
- $25\% = \dfrac{25}{100} = \dfrac{1}{4} = 0.25$.
- **Fraction to percentage**: multiply by 100. $\dfrac{3}{4} \times 100 = 75$, so $\dfrac{3}{4} = 75\%$.
- **Percentage to fraction**: write over 100 and simplify. $24\% = \dfrac{24}{100} = \dfrac{6}{25}$.
- **Percentage to decimal**: divide by 100. $43\% = 0.43$, $5\% = 0.05$, $120\% = 1.2$.
- Percentages make comparison easy because every quantity is put on the same scale of 100.
| Per cent | 50% | 25% | 75% | 20% | 10% | 5% | 1% | $33\tfrac{1}{3}$% |
| Fraction | $\dfrac{1}{2}$ | $\dfrac{1}{4}$ | $\dfrac{3}{4}$ | $\dfrac{1}{5}$ | $\dfrac{1}{10}$ | $\dfrac{1}{20}$ | $\dfrac{1}{100}$ | $\dfrac{1}{3}$ |
| Decimal | 0.5 | 0.25 | 0.75 | 0.2 | 0.1 | 0.05 | 0.01 | 0.333... |
?eg Express $\dfrac{2}{5}$, $\dfrac{72}{150}$ and $\dfrac{1}{3}$ as percentages.
= $\dfrac{2}{5} \times 100 = 40$; $\dfrac{72}{150} \times 100 = 48$; $\dfrac{1}{3} \times 100 = 33.33...$
=> 40%, 48% and $33\tfrac{1}{3}$%
?eg 15 of the 80 students in a school walk to school. What percentage is this?
= $\dfrac{15}{80} \times 100$
=> 18.75%

# Percentage of a quantity
- $y\%$ of a quantity $z$ is $\dfrac{y}{100} \times z$.
- **Mental shortcuts**: 10% is one-tenth; 5% is half of 10%; 20% is double 10%; 25% is a quarter; 15% $=$ 10% $+$ 5%; 75% $=$ 50% $+$ 25%; 90% $=$ 100% $-$ 10%.
- $x\%$ of $y$ equals $y\%$ of $x$, since both are $\dfrac{xy}{100}$. So 12% of 25 $=$ 25% of 12 $= 3$.
- Estimate first. It catches mistakes.

?eg Find 80% of 75 in two ways.
= As a fraction: $\dfrac{4}{5} \times 75 = 60$. As a decimal: $0.8 \times 75 = 60$.
=> 60
?eg A cyclist has covered 92 km, which is 40% of a journey. How far is left?
= 40% is 92 km, so 20% is 46 km and 60% is $92 + 46$.
=> 138 km (the whole journey is 230 km)
?eg 40 is 80% of what number?
= $\dfrac{80}{100} \times n = 40$, so $n = 40 \times \dfrac{100}{80}$
=> 50
?eg Millet and water are mixed in the ratio $2 : 7$. What percentage of the mixture is millet? How much millet is in 450 mL?
= Millet is $\dfrac{2}{9}$ of the mixture: $\dfrac{2}{9} \times 100 \approx 22.2\%$.
= $\dfrac{2}{9} \times 450$
=> About 22.2%; 100 mL

# Percentages greater than 100
- A percentage above 100 means **more than the whole** used for comparison. 120% of a target is 1.2 times the target; 250% is 2.5 times.
?eg A shop aims for sales of ₹5000 a day. It sells goods worth ₹2000 on Monday and ₹6000 on Tuesday. What percentage of the target is each?
= $\dfrac{2000}{5000} \times 100 = 40$ and $\dfrac{6000}{5000} \times 100 = 120$
=> 40% and 120%
?eg A farmer harvested 260 kg last year and 650 kg this year. What percentage of last year's harvest is this year's?
= $\dfrac{650}{260} \times 100$
=> 250%

# Comparing with percentages
- Marks out of different totals, or ingredients in packs of different sizes, are compared by converting to percentages.
- A percentage compares a part with **its own whole**. 35% of a small quantity can be less than 25% of a larger one.
?eg Eesha scores 42 out of 50 in English and 70 out of 80 in Science. In which did she do better?
= English: $\dfrac{42}{50} \times 100 = 84\%$. Science: $\dfrac{70}{80} \times 100 = 87.5\%$.
=> Science
?eg Biscuit A is 25% sugar and biscuit B is 35% sugar. One person eats 120 g of A and another eats 80 g of B. Who eats more sugar?
= A: $0.25 \times 120 = 30$ g. B: $0.35 \times 80 = 28$ g.
=> The person eating A

# Percentage increase and decrease
$$\text{Percentage change} = \frac{\text{amount of change}}{\text{original amount}} \times 100$$
- An increase of $r\%$ multiplies the quantity by $\left(1 + \dfrac{r}{100}\right)$. A decrease of $r\%$ multiplies it by $\left(1 - \dfrac{r}{100}\right)$.
- "Increased by 65%" and "is 165% of the earlier value" say the same thing.
- "Fallen by 85%" means the new value is 15% of the old one.
?eg The price of tomatoes rose from ₹30 to ₹42 per kg. Find the percentage increase.
= Increase $= 12$. $\dfrac{12}{30} \times 100$
=> 40%
?eg The audience in a hall fell from 160 to 100. Find the percentage decrease.
= $\dfrac{60}{160} \times 100$
=> 37.5%
?eg A number increased by 20% becomes 90. Find the number.
= $1.2 \times n = 90$
=> 75

# Profit and loss
- **Cost price (CP)**: what the seller paid. **Selling price (SP)**: what the buyer pays. **Marked price (MP)**: the price quoted before any discount.
- Profit $=$ SP $-$ CP when SP is greater. Loss $=$ CP $-$ SP when CP is greater.
- Profit and loss percentages are taken **on the cost price** unless stated otherwise.
$$\text{Profit}\% = \frac{\text{Profit}}{\text{CP}} \times 100, \qquad \text{Loss}\% = \frac{\text{Loss}}{\text{CP}} \times 100$$
- SP $=$ CP $\times \left(1 + \dfrac{\text{profit}\%}{100}\right)$ or CP $\times \left(1 - \dfrac{\text{loss}\%}{100}\right)$.
- **Gross profit** $=$ sales $-$ cost of the goods. **Net profit** $=$ gross profit $-$ other expenses.
?eg A sweater bought for ₹300 is sold for ₹430. Find the profit percentage.
= Profit $= 130$. $\dfrac{130}{300} \times 100$
=> About 43.3%
?eg Notebooks are bought at ₹36 each and sold at a profit of 20%. Find the selling price.
= $36 \times 1.2$
=> ₹43.20
?eg A box of crayons is sold for ₹50 at a profit of 25%. Find the cost price.
= CP $\times 1.25 = 50$
=> ₹40
?eg A vase bought for ₹2650 is sold at a loss of 18%. Find the selling price.
= $0.82 \times 2650$
=> ₹2173
?eg The selling price of 3 pencils equals the cost price of 5 pencils. Find the profit percentage.
= Let the CP of one pencil be ₹1. Then SP of 3 pencils $= 5$, so SP of one $= \dfrac{5}{3}$.
= Profit on one $= \dfrac{2}{3}$, which is $\dfrac{2}{3} \times 100$ per cent of the cost.
=> $66\tfrac{2}{3}$% profit

# Discount and tax
- **Discount** is a reduction on the marked price. Discount $=$ discount% of MP, and SP $=$ MP $-$ discount.
- **Successive discounts** such as "30% + 20%" are applied one after the other, so they are **not** the same as 50%. The price is multiplied by $0.7 \times 0.8 = 0.56$, a total discount of 44%.
- **Tax** such as GST is a percentage added to the price: final price $=$ price $\times \left(1 + \dfrac{\text{tax}\%}{100}\right)$.
?eg A cooker with a marked price of ₹1800 is sold at a 35% discount. Find the selling price. If it cost the shop ₹900, find the profit percentage.
= SP $= 0.65 \times 1800 = 1170$. Profit $= 270$, and $\dfrac{270}{900} \times 100 = 30$.
=> ₹1170; 30% profit
?eg A car is bought for ₹4,40,000 after a 12% discount. Find the original price.
= $0.88 \times$ MP $= 440000$
=> ₹5,00,000
?eg A phone costs ₹8250 and GST of 18% is added. Find the final price.
= $8250 \times 1.18$
=> ₹9735
?eg A shopkeeper marks goods 50% above cost and then gives a 50% discount. Does he break even?
= SP $= 1.5 \times 0.5 \times$ CP $= 0.75 \times$ CP
=> No. He loses 25%. To break even, the discount must be $33\tfrac{1}{3}$%.

# Interest without compounding
- Money deposited or borrowed is the **principal** $p$. The extra money paid for its use is **interest**. The **rate** $r$ is a percentage per year (per annum, p.a.).
- If the interest is paid out each year, the principal stays the same and the interest each year is the same. After $t$ years:
$$\text{Interest} = p \times r \times t, \qquad \text{Amount} = p(1 + rt)$$
- Here $r$ is written as a decimal: 10% is 0.1.
?eg ₹6000 is deposited at 10% p.a. for 3 years without compounding. Find the total received.
= Interest each year $= 600$. Total interest $= 1800$.
=> ₹7800

# Compounding
- If each year's interest is **added to the principal**, the next year's interest is calculated on a larger amount. This is **compounding**. Each year the amount is multiplied by $(1 + r)$.
$$\text{Amount} = p(1 + r)^t$$
| Year | Start | Interest at 10% | End |
| 1 | 6000 | 600 | 6600 |
| 2 | 6600 | 660 | 7260 |
| 3 | 7260 | 726 | 7986 |
- With compounding ₹6000 becomes ₹7986, a gain of 33.1%. Without it the gain is 30%.
- Growth without compounding is **linear** (adds the same amount each time). Growth with compounding is **exponential** (multiplies by the same factor each time).
- The same formula describes populations and other quantities that grow by a fixed percentage.
?eg ₹20,000 is deposited at 10% p.a. for 2 years. Compare the amounts with and without compounding.
= Without: $20000(1 + 0.1 \times 2) = 24000$. With: $20000 \times 1.1^2 = 24200$.
=> Compounding gives ₹200 more
?eg A city of 1.5 crore people grows by 3% every year. Find the expected population after 2 years.
= $1.5 \times 1.03^2 = 1.5 \times 1.0609$
=> About 1.59 crore
?eg A fare rises by 3% one year and 4% the next. Find the overall percentage increase.
= $1.03 \times 1.04 = 1.0712$
=> 7.12%

# Depreciation and decline
- Many things lose value with age and use. This fall in value is **depreciation**. A fall of $r$ each period multiplies the value by $(1 - r)$.
$$\text{Value after } t \text{ periods} = p(1 - r)^t$$
?eg A TV bought for ₹21,000 loses 5% of its value in a year. Find its value after one year.
= $0.95 \times 21000$
=> ₹19,950
?eg A village of 1250 people shrinks by 10% every decade. Estimate the population after 3 decades.
= $1250 \times 0.9^3 = 1250 \times 0.729$
=> About 911

# Common mistakes
- Adding successive percentages: +10% then +10% is +21%, and 30% then 20% off is 44% off.
- Thinking +50% followed by $-50$% returns to the start. It leaves 75%.
- Using the selling price as the base for profit% or loss%. The base is the cost price.
- Using the new value as the base for a percentage change. The base is the original value.
- Finding the original price by adding the discount percentage to the sale price. Divide instead: MP $=$ SP $\div \left(1 - \dfrac{d}{100}\right)$.
- Comparing percentages of different wholes as if they were actual amounts.
