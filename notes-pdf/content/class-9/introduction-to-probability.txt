@summary Probability measures how likely an event is, on a scale from 0 to 1. This chapter covers experimental and theoretical probability, sample spaces, events and tree diagrams.

# Randomness and the probability scale
- A **random experiment** is an action that can be repeated, whose possible results are known but whose actual result cannot be predicted. Tossing a coin and rolling a die are examples.
- Each performance of the experiment is a **trial**, and each possible result is an **outcome**.
- **Probability** is a number from 0 to 1 that measures how likely an event is. For any event $E$, $0 \leq P(E) \leq 1$.
| Probability | Meaning | Example |
| 0 | Impossible | Getting 7 on an ordinary die |
| Between 0 and $\frac{1}{2}$ | Less likely | Getting 3 on a die |
| $\frac{1}{2}$ | Even chance | Getting heads on a coin |
| Between $\frac{1}{2}$ and 1 | More likely | Getting a number greater than 1 on a die |
| 1 | Certain | Picking a red sweet from a bag of only red sweets |
- A probability can be written as a fraction, a decimal or a percentage: $\dfrac{3}{4} = 0.75 = 75\%$.
- A coin or die is **fair** (unbiased) when no outcome is favoured over another.
- A guess based on personal judgement ("it looks like rain") is a **subjective** probability. This chapter is about measuring probability **objectively**.

# Experimental probability
- Experimental probability is found from actual trials or from recorded data.
$$\text{Experimental probability} = \frac{\text{Number of times the event occurred}}{\text{Total number of trials}}$$
- This fraction is also called the **relative frequency** of the event.
- Different sets of trials can give different values.
?eg A spinner is spun 80 times and stops on red 28 times. Find the experimental probability of red.
= $\dfrac{28}{80} = \dfrac{7}{20}$
=> $0.35$ or 35%

# Theoretical probability
- Theoretical probability is found by reasoning, when all the outcomes are **equally likely**. No experiment is needed.
$$P(E) = \frac{\text{Number of favourable outcomes}}{\text{Number of possible outcomes}}$$
- The event "$E$ does not happen" has probability $P(\text{not } E) = 1 - P(E)$.
?eg A fair die is rolled once. Find the probability of getting (a) a prime number (b) a number that is not prime.
= The primes on a die are 2, 3 and 5: 3 favourable outcomes out of 6.
= $P(\text{not prime}) = 1 - \dfrac{1}{2}$
=> (a) $\dfrac{1}{2}$ (b) $\dfrac{1}{2}$
?eg A letter is picked at random from the word MATHEMATICS. Find the probability that it is (a) M (b) a vowel.
= The word has 11 letters. M occurs twice. The vowels are A, E, A, I: four letters.
=> (a) $\dfrac{2}{11}$ (b) $\dfrac{4}{11}$
?eg A point is chosen at random inside a square of side 4 m. A circle of radius 1 m is drawn inside the square. Find the probability that the point lies inside the circle. Take $\pi = 3.14$.
= When a point is chosen at random in a region, the probability is the ratio of the areas.
= $\dfrac{\pi \times 1^2}{4 \times 4} = \dfrac{3.14}{16}$
=> about 0.196

# Probability from data and samples
- When data has been collected, the relative frequency is used as an estimate of the probability.
- Data is usually collected from a **sample**, a part of the whole **population**. The estimate from the sample is then applied to the population.
- A larger sample that represents all parts of the population gives a more reliable estimate.
?eg In a sample of 60 students, 18 walk to school. Estimate the probability that a student walks to school, and the number who walk in a school of 900 students.
= Probability $\approx \dfrac{18}{60} = 0.3$
= Expected number $= 0.3 \times 900$
=> 0.3; about 270 students

# Experimental and theoretical probability compared
- For a small number of trials, the experimental probability can be quite different from the theoretical probability.
- **Law of Large Numbers**: as the number of trials grows, the experimental probability gets closer to the theoretical probability.
- **Gambler's fallacy**: believing that after several heads in a row, tails is "due". A fair coin has no memory. Each toss is independent, and the probability of tails stays $\dfrac{1}{2}$.
?eg A die is rolled 12 times and shows 3 on three of them. Compare the experimental and theoretical probabilities of getting 3. What do you expect for 6000 rolls?
= Experimental: $\dfrac{3}{12} = 0.25$. Theoretical: $\dfrac{1}{6} \approx 0.167$.
=> They differ because 12 trials are few. For 6000 rolls the experimental value should be close to $\dfrac{1}{6}$, that is about 1000 threes.

# Sample space and events
- The **sample space** $S$ is the list of **all** possible outcomes, each written once. The number of outcomes is written $n(S)$.
- An **event** is any outcome or group of outcomes. It is a subset of the sample space.
- The sample space must suit the question. For "will it rain?", $S = \{\text{Rain}, \text{No rain}\}$ is enough. To study how much it rains, more outcomes are needed.
| Experiment | Sample space | $n(S)$ |
| Tossing a coin | $\{H, T\}$ | 2 |
| Rolling a die | $\{1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6\}$ | 6 |
| Tossing two coins | $\{HH, HT, TH, TT\}$ | 4 |
| A coin and a die together | $\{H1, H2, \ldots, H6, T1, T2, \ldots, T6\}$ | 12 |
?eg A die is rolled. Write the event "a multiple of 3" as a set and find its probability.
= $E = \{3, 6\}$ and $n(S) = 6$
=> $P(E) = \dfrac{2}{6} = \dfrac{1}{3}$
?eg A stall offers 3 snacks (samosa, pakora, bhaji) and 2 drinks (chai, lassi). How many snack-and-drink choices are there? Find the probability that a choice made at random includes lassi.
= Each of the 3 snacks can go with each of the 2 drinks: $3 \times 2 = 6$ choices.
= Three of the six choices include lassi.
=> 6 choices; $\dfrac{3}{6} = \dfrac{1}{2}$

# Tree diagrams
- A **tree diagram** lists the outcomes of an experiment done in steps. Each branch is one possible result of a step, and each path from the start to an end is one outcome.
!fig w=320 h=215; Q s 40 108; Q h 130 58; Q t 130 158; Q hh 225 33; Q ht 225 83; Q th 225 133; Q tt 225 183; L s h; L s t; L h hh; L h ht; L t th; L t tt; T 140 50 H; T 140 176 T; T 245 37 HH; T 245 87 HT; T 245 137 TH; T 245 187 TT; T 85 70 ½; T 85 150 ½; T 30 100 Start; cap Two tosses of a coin give four equally likely outcomes
- To find the probability of one path, **multiply** the probabilities along its branches. To combine several paths, **add** their probabilities.
- **With replacement**: the object is put back, so the probabilities are the same at each step. **Without replacement**: the numbers change after the first draw.
?eg A coin is tossed twice. Find the probability of (a) two heads (b) one head and one tail.
= $S = \{HH, HT, TH, TT\}$
=> (a) $\dfrac{1}{4}$ (b) $\dfrac{2}{4} = \dfrac{1}{2}$
?eg Three coins are tossed. Find the probability of (a) exactly two heads (b) at least one head.
= $S = \{HHH, HHT, HTH, THH, HTT, THT, TTH, TTT\}$, so $n(S) = 8$.
= Exactly two heads: $HHT, HTH, THH$. At least one head: every outcome except $TTT$.
=> (a) $\dfrac{3}{8}$ (b) $\dfrac{7}{8}$
?eg A bag has 2 red and 3 blue balls. A ball is drawn, put back, and a ball is drawn again. Find the probability that the two balls have the same colour.
= $P(\text{red, red}) = \dfrac{2}{5} \times \dfrac{2}{5} = \dfrac{4}{25}$ and $P(\text{blue, blue}) = \dfrac{3}{5} \times \dfrac{3}{5} = \dfrac{9}{25}$
=> $\dfrac{4}{25} + \dfrac{9}{25} = \dfrac{13}{25}$
?eg A bag has 3 red and 2 blue balls. Two balls are drawn one after the other without replacement. Find the probability of (a) red then blue (b) two blue balls.
= (a) $\dfrac{3}{5} \times \dfrac{2}{4} = \dfrac{6}{20}$. After a red ball is removed, 4 balls remain and 2 are blue.
= (b) $\dfrac{2}{5} \times \dfrac{1}{4} = \dfrac{2}{20}$. After a blue ball is removed, only 1 blue ball remains.
=> (a) $\dfrac{3}{10}$ (b) $\dfrac{1}{10}$

# Common mistakes
- Giving a probability greater than 1 or less than 0. Every probability lies from 0 to 1.
- Using the formula for theoretical probability when the outcomes are not equally likely (for example, "the car starts or does not start").
- Treating $HT$ and $TH$ as one outcome. They are different outcomes.
- Counting repeated letters once: in MATHEMATICS there are 11 letters, not 8.
- Forgetting that the total changes when a ball is not put back.
- The gambler's fallacy: past results do not change the probability of the next independent trial.

Number of outcomes for two steps | (outcomes of step 1) $\times$ (outcomes of step 2)

Several paths | Add the probabilities
