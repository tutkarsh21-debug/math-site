@summary Two numbers are enough to fix the position of any point in a plane. This chapter sets up the coordinate plane and uses it to find the distance between two points.

# The Cartesian plane
- A **coordinate system** uses numbers to describe the exact position of a point.
- Two number lines are drawn at right angles. The horizontal line is the **$x$-axis** and the vertical line is the **$y$-axis**. Together they are the **coordinate axes**.
- The axes meet at the **origin** $O$, whose coordinates are $(0, 0)$.
- Equal units are marked on both axes. Distances to the **right** of $O$ and **upwards** from $O$ are positive. Distances to the **left** and **downwards** are negative.
- The plane containing the axes is called the **Cartesian plane**, the **coordinate plane** or the **$xy$-plane**.
!tip The system is named after René Descartes (1637). The negative half of each axis depends on Brahmagupta's work on zero and negative numbers (628 CE).

# Coordinates of a point
- Every point $P$ in the plane is written as $P(x, y)$.
- The **$x$-coordinate** is the perpendicular distance of $P$ from the $y$-axis, measured along the $x$-axis.
- The **$y$-coordinate** is the perpendicular distance of $P$ from the $x$-axis, measured along the $y$-axis.
- The order matters. If $x \neq y$, then $(x, y) \neq (y, x)$. The two are the same point only when $x = y$.
- A point on the $x$-axis has the form $(x, 0)$. A point on the $y$-axis has the form $(0, y)$.
?eg Write the coordinates of the point that is (a) on the $x$-axis, 4.5 units to the right of $O$ (b) on the $y$-axis, 4.5 units below $O$.
= (a) It is on the $x$-axis, so $y = 0$. To the right means $x$ is positive.
= (b) It is on the $y$-axis, so $x = 0$. Below means $y$ is negative.
=> (a) $(4.5, 0)$ (b) $(0, -4.5)$

# The four quadrants
- The axes divide the plane into four parts called **quadrants**, numbered anticlockwise from the top right.

| Quadrant | Sign of $x$ | Sign of $y$ | Example |
| I | $+$ | $+$ | $(3, 2)$ |
| II | $-$ | $+$ | $(-5, 3)$ |
| III | $-$ | $-$ | $(-3, -3)$ |
| IV | $+$ | $-$ | $(3, -5)$ |
- A point on an axis does not belong to any quadrant.
?eg In which quadrant, or on which axis, does each point lie: $(-5, 3)$, $(3, -5)$, $(0, 4)$, $(-2.9, 0)$?
= $(-5, 3)$: $x$ negative, $y$ positive. $(3, -5)$: $x$ positive, $y$ negative.
= $(0, 4)$ has $x = 0$ and $(-2.9, 0)$ has $y = 0$.
=> Quadrant II; Quadrant IV; on the $y$-axis; on the $x$-axis

# Distance along a line parallel to an axis
- Two points with the same $y$-coordinate lie on a line parallel to the $x$-axis. The distance between $(x_1, y)$ and $(x_2, y)$ is $|x_2 - x_1|$.
- Two points with the same $x$-coordinate lie on a line parallel to the $y$-axis. The distance between $(x, y_1)$ and $(x, y_2)$ is $|y_2 - y_1|$.
- The bars mean the **absolute value**: take the difference and ignore its sign.
?eg A door has its two ends at $(8.5, 0)$ and $(11.5, 0)$. How wide is it? Another door has ends at $(0, 1.5)$ and $(0, 4)$. Which door is wider?
= First door: $|11.5 - 8.5| = 3$ units. Second door: $|4 - 1.5| = 2.5$ units.
=> The first door is 3 units wide and is the wider one.
?eg Three corners of a rectangular table are at $(8, 9)$, $(11, 9)$ and $(11, 7)$. Find the fourth corner and the length and width of the table.
= The fourth corner has the $x$-coordinate of the first point and the $y$-coordinate of the third.
= Length $= |11 - 8| = 3$ units and width $= |9 - 7| = 2$ units.
=> Fourth corner $(8, 7)$; the table is 3 units by 2 units.

# The distance formula
- For a slanting segment, draw a right-angled triangle with sides parallel to the axes and use the **Baudhāyana–Pythagoras theorem**.
!fig w=320 h=200; P A 60 160 bl; P D 240 50 tr; P F 240 160 br; L A F; L F D; H A D; R A F D; T 150 178 x₂ − x₁; T 275 110 y₂ − y₁; cap AD is the hypotenuse of a right-angled triangle
- The distance between $A(x_1, y_1)$ and $D(x_2, y_2)$ is
$$AD = \sqrt{(x_2 - x_1)^2 + (y_2 - y_1)^2}$$
- It makes no difference whether $x_2 - x_1$ and $y_2 - y_1$ are positive or negative, because they are squared.
- The distance of $(x, y)$ from the origin is $\sqrt{x^2 + y^2}$.
?eg Find the lengths of the sides of the triangle with vertices $A(3, 4)$, $D(7, 1)$ and $M(9, 6)$.
= $AD = \sqrt{(7 - 3)^2 + (1 - 4)^2} = \sqrt{16 + 9} = 5$ units
= $DM = \sqrt{(9 - 7)^2 + (6 - 1)^2} = \sqrt{4 + 25} = \sqrt{29}$ units
= $MA = \sqrt{(3 - 9)^2 + (4 - 6)^2} = \sqrt{36 + 4} = \sqrt{40}$ units
=> $AD = 5$, $DM = \sqrt{29}$ and $MA = \sqrt{40}$ units
?eg Show that $A(2, 1)$, $B(-1, 2)$, $C(-2, -1)$ and $D(1, -2)$ are the vertices of a square, and find its area.
= $AB^2 = 9 + 1 = 10$, $BC^2 = 1 + 9 = 10$, $CD^2 = 9 + 1 = 10$, $DA^2 = 1 + 9 = 10$, so all four sides are equal.
= Diagonals: $AC^2 = 16 + 4 = 20$ and $BD^2 = 4 + 16 = 20$, so the diagonals are equal.
= Four equal sides and equal diagonals make a square. Area $= (\sqrt{10})^2$.
=> $ABCD$ is a square of area 10 square units.

# Reflection in the axes
- Reflecting a point in the **$y$-axis** changes the sign of its $x$-coordinate: $(x, y) \to (-x, y)$.
- Reflecting a point in the **$x$-axis** changes the sign of its $y$-coordinate: $(x, y) \to (x, -y)$.
- A reflection does not change lengths. A triangle and its image have equal sides.
?eg Triangle $ADM$ with $A(3, 4)$, $D(7, 1)$, $M(9, 6)$ is reflected in the $y$-axis. Write the images and check that $A'D' = AD$.
= $A'(-3, 4)$, $D'(-7, 1)$ and $M'(-9, 6)$
= $A'D' = \sqrt{(-7 + 3)^2 + (1 - 4)^2} = \sqrt{16 + 9} = 5$ units, the same as $AD$.
=> The reflection keeps the lengths of the sides.

# Using distances: collinear points, mid-points and circles
- **Collinear points**: three points lie on one line if the largest of the three distances between them equals the sum of the other two.
- **Mid-point**: the mid-point $M$ of the segment joining $S(x_1, y_1)$ and $T(x_2, y_2)$ is
$$M = \left(\frac{x_1 + x_2}{2},\ \frac{y_1 + y_2}{2}\right)$$
- **Circle**: a point lies on a circle with centre $C$ and radius $r$ if its distance from $C$ equals $r$. It lies inside if the distance is less than $r$ and outside if it is greater.
?eg Are the points $M(-3, -4)$, $A(0, 0)$ and $G(6, 8)$ on one straight line?
= $MA = \sqrt{9 + 16} = 5$, $AG = \sqrt{36 + 64} = 10$, $MG = \sqrt{81 + 144} = 15$
= $MA + AG = 5 + 10 = 15 = MG$
=> Yes, the three points are collinear.
?eg $M(-7, 1)$ is the mid-point of $A(3, -4)$ and $B(x, y)$. Find $B$.
= $\dfrac{3 + x}{2} = -7$ gives $x = -17$, and $\dfrac{-4 + y}{2} = 1$ gives $y = 6$.
=> $B = (-17, 6)$
?eg Show that $A(1, -8)$, $B(-4, 7)$ and $C(-7, -4)$ lie on a circle with centre at the origin. Does $D(-5, 6)$ lie inside, on or outside this circle?
= $OA = \sqrt{1 + 64} = \sqrt{65}$, $OB = \sqrt{16 + 49} = \sqrt{65}$, $OC = \sqrt{49 + 16} = \sqrt{65}$
= All three are at the same distance from $O$, so they lie on a circle of radius $\sqrt{65}$.
= $OD = \sqrt{25 + 36} = \sqrt{61}$, which is less than $\sqrt{65}$.
=> The radius is $\sqrt{65}$ units, and $D$ lies inside the circle.

# Common mistakes
- Writing the coordinates in the wrong order. The $x$-coordinate always comes first.
- Thinking a point on an axis is in a quadrant. It is on the boundary and belongs to none.
- Sign errors with negative coordinates: $-3 - (-7) = 4$, not $-10$.
- Forgetting to square before adding in the distance formula, or forgetting the square root at the end.
- Saying three points are collinear because two of the distances are equal. Check that two distances **add up** to the third.
