Q: [(i)] $D_1R_1$ is the door of the room. How far is the door from the left wall (the $y$-axis), and how far is it from the $x$-axis?
S: The door starts at $D_1$, which is 8 units to the right of $O$. So the door is 8 ft from the left wall.
S: Both $D_1$ and $R_1$ lie on the $x$-axis, so the distance of the door from the $x$-axis is 0.
S: **Answer: 8 ft from the $y$-axis and 0 ft from the $x$-axis**
Q: [(ii)] Write the coordinates of $D_1$.
S: $D_1$ is on the $x$-axis, 8 units from $O$, so its $y$-coordinate is 0.
S: **Answer: $D_1 = (8, 0)$**
Q: [(iii)] $R_1$ is the point $(11.5, 0)$. How wide is the door? Is this width comfortable, and can a person in a wheelchair enter easily?
S: Both points are on the $x$-axis, so the width is the difference of the $x$-coordinates: $11.5 - 8 = 3.5$ ft.
S: This is a comfortable width for a room door. A wheelchair is a little over 2 ft wide, so it can pass through a 3.5 ft door easily.
S: **Answer: 3.5 ft, which is wide enough for a wheelchair**
Q: [(iv)] $B_1(0, 1.5)$ and $B_2(0, 4)$ are the ends of the bathroom door. Is it narrower or wider than the room door?
S: Both points are on the $y$-axis, so the width is the difference of the $y$-coordinates: $4 - 1.5 = 2.5$ ft.
S: $2.5 < 3.5$, so the bathroom door is narrower than the room door by 1 ft.
S: **Answer: narrower (2.5 ft against 3.5 ft)**

Q: [Q1] A rectangular study table has three of its feet at $(8, 9)$, $(11, 9)$ and $(11, 7)$. (i) Where is the fourth foot? (ii) Is this a good spot for the table? (iii) What are the width and the length of the table? Can its height be found?
S: (i) The feet form a rectangle with sides parallel to the axes. The $x$-coordinates used are 8 and 11 and the $y$-coordinates are 9 and 7. The missing combination is $(8, 7)$.
S: (ii) The table lies between $x = 8$ and $x = 11$ and between $y = 7$ and $y = 9$. It is clear of the bed (which ends at $x = 6$), of the wardrobe and of the door on the bottom wall, and it is close to the corner $B$. So it is a sensible spot. Other answers are possible if you give a reason.
S: (iii) Length $= 11 - 8 = 3$ ft and width $= 9 - 7 = 2$ ft. The height cannot be found, because the plan shows the room from above and has no third coordinate.
S: **Answer: (i) $(8, 7)$ (ii) yes (iii) 3 ft long and 2 ft wide; the height cannot be found**
Q: [Q2] The bathroom door $B_1B_2$ has its hinge at $B_1$ and opens into the bedroom. Will it hit the wardrobe? What would you change if the door were made wider?
S: The door is $4 - 1.5 = 2.5$ ft wide. As it opens, its free end moves on a circle of radius 2.5 ft with centre $B_1(0, 1.5)$.
S: The nearest point of the wardrobe to $B_1$ is $(3, 1.5)$ on the side $W_1W_4$, which is 3 ft away. Since $2.5 < 3$, the door does not reach the wardrobe.
S: A door of width 3 ft or more would hit it. Then the door could be hinged at $B_2$, made to open into the bathroom, or the wardrobe could be moved to the right.

T: End-of-Chapter Exercises
Q: [Q1] What are the $x$-coordinate and the $y$-coordinate of the point where the two axes meet?
S: The axes meet at the origin, which is at zero distance from both axes.
S: **Answer: both are 0, so the point is $(0, 0)$**
Q: [Q2] The point $W$ has $x$-coordinate $-5$. What can you say about the coordinates of a point $H$ on the line through $W$ parallel to the $y$-axis? In which quadrants can $H$ lie?
S: Every point on a line parallel to the $y$-axis has the same $x$-coordinate. So $H = (-5, y)$ for some number $y$.
S: If $y > 0$, $H$ is in Quadrant II. If $y < 0$, $H$ is in Quadrant III. If $y = 0$, $H$ is on the $x$-axis.
S: **Answer: $H = (-5, y)$, in Quadrant II or Quadrant III**
Q: [Q3] The points $R(3, 0)$, $A(0, -2)$, $M(-5, -2)$ and $P(-5, 2)$ are joined in order. Predict (i) two sides of $RAMP$ that are perpendicular (ii) a side that is parallel to an axis (iii) two points that are mirror images in an axis, and name the axis.
S: (i) $A$ and $M$ have the same $y$-coordinate, so $AM$ is horizontal. $M$ and $P$ have the same $x$-coordinate, so $MP$ is vertical. So $AM \perp MP$.
S: (ii) $AM$ is parallel to the $x$-axis (and $MP$ is parallel to the $y$-axis).
S: (iii) $M(-5, -2)$ and $P(-5, 2)$ differ only in the sign of the $y$-coordinate, so they are mirror images in the $x$-axis.

S: **Answer: (i) $AM$ and $MP$ (ii) $AM$ (iii) $M$ and $P$, in the $x$-axis**
Q: [Q4] Plot $Z(5, -6)$. Construct a right-angled triangle $IZN$ and find the lengths of its three sides.
S: One possible answer: take $N(1, -6)$ and $I(5, -3)$. Then $ZN$ is horizontal and $ZI$ is vertical, so the right angle is at $Z$.
S: $ZN = 5 - 1 = 4$ units and $ZI = -3 - (-6) = 3$ units. By the Baudhayana-Pythagoras theorem, $IN = \sqrt{4^2 + 3^2} = 5$ units.
S: **Answer: sides 3, 4 and 5 units for this choice. Other triangles are also correct.**
Q: [Q5] What would a system of coordinates be like without negative numbers? Could it locate every point of the plane?
S: With only zero and positive numbers, we could give coordinates only to the origin, the positive halves of the two axes and the points of Quadrant I.
S: Points to the left of the $y$-axis or below the $x$-axis could not be named, so the system would not locate all the points of the plane.
S: **Answer: only Quadrant I and its edges could be located, so not every point**
Q: [Q6*] Are $M(-3, -4)$, $A(0, 0)$ and $G(6, 8)$ on one straight line? Give a method that does not need plotting.
S: Method: find the three distances. Three points lie on one line exactly when the two shorter distances add up to the longest.
S: $MA = \sqrt{3^2 + 4^2} = 5$, $AG = \sqrt{6^2 + 8^2} = 10$ and $MG = \sqrt{9^2 + 12^2} = \sqrt{225} = 15$.
S: $MA + AG = 5 + 10 = 15 = MG$.
S: **Answer: yes, the three points are on one straight line**
Q: [Q7*] Use the same method to check whether $R(-5, -1)$, $B(-2, -5)$ and $C(4, -12)$ are on one straight line.
S: $RB = \sqrt{3^2 + 4^2} = 5$, $BC = \sqrt{6^2 + 7^2} = \sqrt{85} \approx 9.22$ and $RC = \sqrt{9^2 + 11^2} = \sqrt{202} \approx 14.21$.
S: $RB + BC \approx 14.22$, which is close to $RC$ but not equal to it. To be sure: if $5 + \sqrt{85} = \sqrt{202}$, squaring gives $110 + 10\sqrt{85} = 202$, so $\sqrt{85} = 9.2$ and $85 = 84.64$, which is false.
S: **Answer: no. The points are nearly, but not exactly, in a line, which a plotted graph may not show clearly.**
Q: [Q8*] Take the origin as one vertex and give the vertices of (i) a right-angled isosceles triangle (ii) an isosceles triangle whose other two vertices lie in Quadrant III and Quadrant IV.
S: (i) One possible answer: $O(0, 0)$, $(4, 0)$ and $(0, 4)$. The two sides along the axes are 4 units each and meet at a right angle at $O$.
S: (ii) One possible answer: $O(0, 0)$, $(-3, -4)$ and $(3, -4)$. Each of the first two sides is $\sqrt{3^2 + 4^2} = 5$ units, so the triangle is isosceles.
S: **Answer: (i) $(0, 0)$, $(4, 0)$, $(0, 4)$ (ii) $(0, 0)$, $(-3, -4)$, $(3, -4)$. Other answers are possible.**
Q: [Q9*] For each row, say whether $M$ is the mid-point of $ST$, with a reason. What connects the coordinates of $M$, $S$ and $T$ when $M$ is the mid-point?
| $S$ | $M$ | $T$ |
| $(-3, 0)$ | $(0, 0)$ | $(3, 0)$ |
| $(2, 3)$ | $(3, 4)$ | $(4, 5)$ |
| $(0, 0)$ | $(0, 5)$ | $(0, -10)$ |
| $(-8, 7)$ | $(0, -2)$ | $(6, -3)$ |
S: $M$ is the mid-point when it is on the segment and $SM = MT$.
| $S$, $M$, $T$ | Mid-point? | Reason |
| $(-3, 0)$, $(0, 0)$, $(3, 0)$ | Yes | All on the $x$-axis, and $SM = MT = 3$ |
| $(2, 3)$, $(3, 4)$, $(4, 5)$ | Yes | $SM = MT = \sqrt{2}$ and $ST = 2\sqrt{2}$ |
| $(0, 0)$, $(0, 5)$, $(0, -10)$ | No | $M$ is above $S$ but $T$ is below it, so $M$ is not between them |
| $(-8, 7)$, $(0, -2)$, $(6, -3)$ | No | $SM = \sqrt{145}$ but $MT = \sqrt{37}$ |
S: In the two "Yes" rows, each coordinate of $M$ is the average of the matching coordinates of $S$ and $T$: $M = \left(\dfrac{x_1 + x_2}{2}, \dfrac{y_1 + y_2}{2}\right)$.
S: **Answer: Yes, Yes, No, No. The mid-point has the averages of the coordinates.**
Q: [Q10*] $M(-7, 1)$ is the mid-point of $A(3, -4)$ and $B(x, y)$. Find $B$.
S: $\dfrac{3 + x}{2} = -7$ gives $x = -14 - 3 = -17$.
S: $\dfrac{-4 + y}{2} = 1$ gives $y = 2 + 4 = 6$.
S: **Answer: $B = (-17, 6)$**
Q: [Q11*] $P$ and $Q$ trisect $AB$, with $P$ nearer to $A$. Using mid-points, find $P$ and $Q$ when $A = (4, 7)$ and $B = (16, -2)$.
S: Since $AP = PQ = QB$, $P$ is the mid-point of $AQ$ and $Q$ is the mid-point of $PB$. Let $P = (a, b)$ and $Q = (c, d)$.
S: $x$-coordinates: $2a = 4 + c$ and $2c = a + 16$. Putting $c = 2a - 4$ in the second: $4a - 8 = a + 16$, so $a = 8$ and $c = 12$.
S: $y$-coordinates: $2b = 7 + d$ and $2d = b - 2$. Putting $d = 2b - 7$ in the second: $4b - 14 = b - 2$, so $b = 4$ and $d = 1$.
S: **Answer: $P = (8, 4)$ and $Q = (12, 1)$**
Q: [Q12*] (i) Show that $A(1, -8)$, $B(-4, 7)$ and $C(-7, -4)$ lie on a circle $K$ with centre $O(0, 0)$, and find its radius. (ii) Are $D(-5, 6)$ and $E(0, 9)$ inside, on or outside $K$?
S: (i) $OA = \sqrt{1 + 64} = \sqrt{65}$, $OB = \sqrt{16 + 49} = \sqrt{65}$ and $OC = \sqrt{49 + 16} = \sqrt{65}$. The three points are at the same distance from $O$, so they lie on a circle with centre $O$ and radius $\sqrt{65} \approx 8.06$ units.
S: (ii) $OD = \sqrt{25 + 36} = \sqrt{61} < \sqrt{65}$, so $D$ is inside the circle. $OE = 9 = \sqrt{81} > \sqrt{65}$, so $E$ is outside the circle.
S: **Answer: (i) radius $\sqrt{65}$ units (ii) $D$ is inside and $E$ is outside**
Q: [Q13*] The mid-points of the sides of $\triangle ABC$ are $D(5, 1)$, $E(6, 5)$ and $F(0, 3)$. Find $A$, $B$ and $C$.
S: Take $D$, $E$ and $F$ as the mid-points of $BC$, $CA$ and $AB$. Adding the coordinates in pairs: $B + C = 2D = (10, 2)$, $C + A = 2E = (12, 10)$ and $A + B = 2F = (0, 6)$.
S: Adding all three, $2(A + B + C) = (22, 18)$, so $A + B + C = (11, 9)$.
S: $A = (11, 9) - (10, 2) = (1, 7)$, $B = (11, 9) - (12, 10) = (-1, -1)$ and $C = (11, 9) - (0, 6) = (11, 3)$.
S: **Answer: $A(1, 7)$, $B(-1, -1)$, $C(11, 3)$**
Q: [Q14] Two main roads of a city cross at its centre, one running North-South and the other East-West. All other streets are parallel to these and 200 m apart, with 10 streets in each direction. (i) Draw a model with 1 cm $=$ 200 m. (ii) The crossing of the 2nd North-South street and the 5th East-West street is called $(2, 5)$. How many crossings can be called (a) $(4, 3)$ (b) $(3, 4)$?
S: (i) Draw 10 vertical lines 1 cm apart for the North-South streets and 10 horizontal lines 1 cm apart for the East-West streets, and number each set from 1 to 10.
S: (ii) Two streets in different directions cross at exactly one point. So only one crossing is $(4, 3)$: the 4th North-South street with the 3rd East-West street. In the same way only one crossing is $(3, 4)$.
S: These are two different crossings, because the order of the two numbers matters.
S: **Answer: (a) one (b) one**
Q: [Q15] A screen is 800 pixels wide and 600 pixels high, with the origin at its bottom-left corner. One circular icon has centre $A(100, 150)$ and radius 80. Another has centre $B(250, 230)$ and radius 100. (i) Does any part of either circle lie outside the screen? (ii) Do the circles intersect?
S: (i) The first circle runs from $x = 20$ to $x = 180$ and from $y = 70$ to $y = 230$. The second runs from $x = 150$ to $x = 350$ and from $y = 130$ to $y = 330$. All of these are between 0 and 800 for $x$ and between 0 and 600 for $y$, so both circles are fully on the screen.
S: (ii) $AB = \sqrt{150^2 + 80^2} = \sqrt{28900} = 170$. The sum of the radii is $80 + 100 = 180$ and their difference is 20.
S: Since $20 < 170 < 180$, the circles cut each other at two points.
S: **Answer: (i) no (ii) yes, they intersect**
Q: [Q16] Plot $A(2, 1)$, $B(-1, 2)$, $C(-2, -1)$ and $D(1, -2)$. Is $ABCD$ a square? Why? Find its area.
S: Sides: $AB = \sqrt{3^2 + 1^2} = \sqrt{10}$, $BC = \sqrt{1^2 + 3^2} = \sqrt{10}$, $CD = \sqrt{10}$ and $DA = \sqrt{10}$. All four sides are equal.
S: Diagonals: $AC = \sqrt{4^2 + 2^2} = \sqrt{20}$ and $BD = \sqrt{2^2 + 4^2} = \sqrt{20}$. The diagonals are equal too.
S: A quadrilateral with four equal sides and equal diagonals is a square. Its area is $(\sqrt{10})^2 = 10$ square units.
