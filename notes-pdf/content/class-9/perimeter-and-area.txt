@summary This chapter measures the boundary and the region of plane shapes: the circumference and arcs of a circle, the areas of triangles and quadrilaterals (including Heron's and Brahmagupta's formulas), and the areas of circles, sectors and segments.

# Perimeter and the number π
- The **perimeter** of a shape is the total length of its boundary. Square of side $a$: $4a$. Equilateral triangle of side $a$: $3a$. Rectangle with sides $a$ and $b$: $2(a + b)$.
- For every square the ratio perimeter : side is $4 : 1$, whatever its size. In the same way, for every circle the ratio **circumference : diameter** is the same number. This number is called $\pi$.
$$\pi = \frac{C}{D} \qquad C = \pi d = 2\pi r$$
- $\pi$ is **irrational**: its decimal form $3.14159265\ldots$ never ends and never repeats (proved by Lambert in 1761). So $\pi \approx \dfrac{22}{7}$ and $\pi \approx 3.14$, but $\pi \neq \dfrac{22}{7}$.
| Who | Value or result for π |
| Mesopotamia (c. 1900 BCE) | $3\dfrac{1}{8} = 3.125$ |
| Archimedes (c. 250 BCE) | $3\dfrac{10}{71} < \pi < 3\dfrac{1}{7}$ |
| Zu Chongzhi (480 CE) | $\dfrac{22}{7}$ and $\dfrac{355}{113} \approx 3.1415929$ |
| Āryabhaṭa (499 CE) | $\dfrac{62832}{20000} = 3.1416$, called "approximate" |
| Brahmagupta (628 CE) | $\sqrt{10} \approx 3.162$ |
| Mādhava (14th century) | $\dfrac{\pi}{4} = 1 - \dfrac{1}{3} + \dfrac{1}{5} - \dfrac{1}{7} + \ldots$, an exact formula |
!tip A regular hexagon drawn inside a circle of radius 1 has perimeter 6, which is less than the circumference $2\pi$. This shows $\pi > 3$.
?eg A wheel has a diameter of 70 cm. How far does it move in one turn, and how many turns does it make in 1.1 km? Take $\pi = \dfrac{22}{7}$.
= One turn $=$ circumference $= \dfrac{22}{7} \times 70 = 220$ cm
= $1.1$ km $= 110000$ cm, so the number of turns is $\dfrac{110000}{220}$.
=> 220 cm; 500 turns

# Length of an arc
- A semicircle is half the circle, so its length is $\pi r$. A quarter circle has length $\dfrac{\pi r}{2}$.
- An arc that subtends an angle $\theta°$ at the centre is the fraction $\dfrac{\theta}{360}$ of the circle:
$$\text{arc length} = \frac{\theta}{360} \times 2\pi r$$
- The **perimeter of a sector** is the arc length plus two radii.
- On a running track, a runner in an outer lane runs semicircles of larger radius. If the lanes are $w$ metres apart, one full lap in the next lane is $2\pi w$ metres longer. The **stagger** at the start makes up for this.
?eg Find the length of an arc of a circle of radius 21 cm that subtends $120°$ at the centre. Take $\pi = \dfrac{22}{7}$.
= Arc $= \dfrac{120}{360} \times 2 \times \dfrac{22}{7} \times 21 = \dfrac{1}{3} \times 132$
=> 44 cm
?eg Find the perimeter of a sector of radius 14 cm and angle $90°$. Take $\pi = \dfrac{22}{7}$.
= Arc $= \dfrac{90}{360} \times 2 \times \dfrac{22}{7} \times 14 = 22$ cm
= Perimeter $= 22 + 14 + 14$
=> 50 cm
?eg A semicircle is drawn on a segment $PQ$. Three smaller semicircles are drawn on three parts of $PQ$ that together make up $PQ$. Which path from $P$ to $Q$ is longer?
= If the large radius is $a$ and the small radii are $b$, $c$, $d$, then $2a = 2b + 2c + 2d$, so $a = b + c + d$.
= Large path $= \pi a$. Small paths together $= \pi b + \pi c + \pi d = \pi(b + c + d)$.
=> The two paths are equal in length.

# Area of rectangles, parallelograms and triangles
- The unit of area is a square of side 1 unit. Rectangle: $ab$. Square: $a^2$.
- **Parallelogram**: cutting a right-angled triangle from one end and moving it to the other turns it into a rectangle with the same base and height. So area $=$ base $\times$ height $= bh$.
- The sides alone do not fix the area of a parallelogram. The height matters.
- **Triangle**: two congruent copies of a triangle fit together to make a parallelogram with the same base and height. So area $= \dfrac{1}{2} \times$ base $\times$ height.
- **Theorem**: a **median** of a triangle divides it into two triangles of equal area, because they have equal bases and the same height.
!fig w=300 h=200; P A 120 25 t; P B 40 170 bl; P C 260 170 br; P D 150 170 b; F A B D; L A B; L B C; L C A; H A D; T 95 188 BD; T 205 188 DC; cap AD is a median. BD = DC, so the two triangles have equal area.
- **Trapezium** with parallel sides $a$, $b$ and height $h$: area $= \dfrac{1}{2}(a + b)h$.
- **Rhombus or kite** with diagonals $d_1$ and $d_2$: area $= \dfrac{1}{2}d_1d_2$.
?eg The parallel sides of an isosceles trapezium are 30 cm and 14 cm, and each of the other sides is 17 cm. Find its area.
= Dropping perpendiculars from the ends of the shorter side leaves $\dfrac{30 - 14}{2} = 8$ cm at each end of the longer side.
= Height $= \sqrt{17^2 - 8^2} = 15$ cm. Area $= \dfrac{1}{2}(30 + 14) \times 15$.
=> 330 cm²

# Heron's formula
- For a triangle with sides $a$, $b$, $c$, first find the **semi-perimeter** $s = \dfrac{a + b + c}{2}$. Then
$$\text{Area} = \sqrt{s(s - a)(s - b)(s - c)}$$
- For an **equilateral triangle** of side $a$ this gives $\dfrac{\sqrt{3}}{4}a^2$, the same as half base times height with height $\dfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}a$.
- Two more formulas: if $R$ is the radius of the circumcircle and $r$ the radius of the incircle, then Area $= \dfrac{abc}{4R}$ and Area $= r \times s$.
?eg Find the area of a triangle with sides 13 cm, 14 cm and 15 cm. Then find the radius of its incircle.
= $s = \dfrac{13 + 14 + 15}{2} = 21$
= Area $= \sqrt{21 \times 8 \times 7 \times 6} = \sqrt{7056} = 84$ cm²
= Area $= r \times s$, so $r = \dfrac{84}{21}$.
=> Area 84 cm²; inradius 4 cm
?eg The sides of a triangular plot are in the ratio $3 : 4 : 5$ and its perimeter is 96 m. Find its area.
= The sides are $3k$, $4k$, $5k$ with $12k = 96$, so $k = 8$: the sides are 24 m, 32 m and 40 m.
= $s = 48$. Area $= \sqrt{48 \times 24 \times 16 \times 8} = \sqrt{147456} = 384$
=> 384 m² (check: it is right-angled, and $\dfrac{1}{2} \times 24 \times 32 = 384$)

# Brahmagupta's formula for a cyclic quadrilateral
- The four sides alone do not fix the area of a quadrilateral: a rhombus of side 3 can be pushed flatter and flatter. One more fact is needed.
- If the quadrilateral is **cyclic**, with sides $a$, $b$, $c$, $d$ and semi-perimeter $s = \dfrac{a + b + c + d}{2}$, then (Brahmagupta, 628 CE)
$$\text{Area} = \sqrt{(s - a)(s - b)(s - c)(s - d)}$$
- A triangle is the special case $d = 0$: the formula then becomes Heron's formula. So Brahmagupta's formula **generalises** Heron's formula.
- Check with a rectangle of sides $a$, $b$: $s = a + b$, and the formula gives $\sqrt{b \cdot a \cdot b \cdot a} = ab$.
?eg A cyclic quadrilateral has sides 7 cm, 24 cm, 20 cm and 15 cm. Find its area.
= $s = \dfrac{7 + 24 + 20 + 15}{2} = 33$
= Area $= \sqrt{26 \times 9 \times 13 \times 18} = \sqrt{234 \times 234}$
=> 234 cm²

# Squaring a rectangle
- To **square** a shape means to construct a square with the same area.
- Baudhāyana's construction for a rectangle with sides $a$ and $b$ uses the identity
$$ab = \left(\frac{a + b}{2}\right)^2 - \left(\frac{a - b}{2}\right)^2$$
- In a right-angled triangle with hypotenuse $\dfrac{a + b}{2}$ and one side $\dfrac{a - b}{2}$, the third side $x$ has $x^2 = ab$. A square on that side has the same area as the rectangle.
?eg Find the side of the square equal in area to a rectangle 9 cm by 4 cm, using the identity.
= $\left(\dfrac{13}{2}\right)^2 - \left(\dfrac{5}{2}\right)^2 = \dfrac{169 - 25}{4} = 36$
=> The side is 6 cm.

# Area of a circle
- For any shape, (perimeter)² : area stays the same when the shape is enlarged. For a circle the area is proportional to $r^2$, and Archimedes showed that the constant is exactly $\pi$.
$$A = \pi r^2 = \frac{1}{2} \times \text{circumference} \times \text{radius}$$
- Nīlakaṇṭha's picture: cut the circle into many thin slices and fit them together alternately. They form nearly a parallelogram with base $\pi r$ (half the circumference) and height $r$, so the area is $\pi r \times r$.
?eg Find the area of a circle whose circumference is 44 cm. Take $\pi = \dfrac{22}{7}$.
= $2 \times \dfrac{22}{7} \times r = 44$, so $r = 7$ cm.
= Area $= \dfrac{22}{7} \times 49$
=> 154 cm²

# Sector and segment of a circle
- A **sector** is the region between two radii and the arc joining their ends. A **segment** is the region between a chord and its arc.
!fig w=250 h=215; P O 110 115 l; P A 185.2 87.6 r; P B 123.9 36.2 t; P P 34.8 142.4 l; P Q 150 184.3 br; C O 80; SEC O 80 20 80; SEG O 80 200 300; L O A; L O B; L P Q; T 150 80 sector; T 95 178 segment
$$\text{Area of a sector} = \frac{\theta}{360} \times \pi r^2$$
- Area of a **minor segment** $=$ area of the sector $-$ area of the triangle formed by the two radii and the chord.
- Area of the **major** sector or segment $=$ area of the circle $-$ area of the minor one.
?eg Find the area of a sector of radius 21 cm and angle $120°$. Take $\pi = \dfrac{22}{7}$.
= Area $= \dfrac{120}{360} \times \dfrac{22}{7} \times 21 \times 21 = \dfrac{1}{3} \times 1386$
=> 462 cm²
?eg A chord of a circle of radius 14 cm subtends $90°$ at the centre. Find the area of the minor segment. Take $\pi = \dfrac{22}{7}$.
= Sector $= \dfrac{90}{360} \times \dfrac{22}{7} \times 14 \times 14 = 154$ cm²
= Triangle $= \dfrac{1}{2} \times 14 \times 14 = 98$ cm² (the two radii are at right angles)
=> Segment $= 154 - 98 = 56$ cm²
?eg The minute hand of a clock is 14 cm long. Find the area it sweeps in 15 minutes.
= In 15 minutes the hand turns through $\dfrac{15}{60} \times 360° = 90°$.
= Area $= \dfrac{90}{360} \times \dfrac{22}{7} \times 14 \times 14$
=> 154 cm²

# Common mistakes
- Using the diameter in place of the radius in $\pi r^2$ or $2\pi r$.
- Giving the arc length as the perimeter of a sector. Add the two radii.
- Forgetting to halve the perimeter before using Heron's or Brahmagupta's formula.
- Using Brahmagupta's formula for a quadrilateral that is not cyclic.
- Writing $\pi = \dfrac{22}{7}$ as an exact value. It is an approximation.
- Mixing units: change km to cm (or m) before dividing a distance by a circumference.
